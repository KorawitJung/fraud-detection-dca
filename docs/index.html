When do scammers move money, and how much can a bank stop?

Four plain questions about fraud in mobile-money transfers, answered with one month of simulated transactions (PaySim).

Q1

Which kinds of transaction do fraudsters use?

Only two: transfers and cash-outs.

How to read this chart
Each bar is one transaction type. Its length is the share of that type that turned out to be fraud. Red bars have fraud; grey bars have none.
What the data says
TRANSFER is the riskiest type: 0.77% of them are fraud (4,097 cases). CASH_OUT follows at 0.18% (4,116 cases). CASH_IN, DEBIT, PAYMENT have no fraud at all.
What it means
Fraud here is about moving money out: send it to another account, then withdraw it as cash. That is the same two-step pattern as mule-account scams, so monitoring can concentrate on these two transaction types.

Q2

What time of day does fraud happen?

Fraud runs around the clock. Normal customers do not.

How to read this chart
Top: blue bars show when all transfers and cash-outs happen during the day; red bars show when fraud happens. Each colour adds up to 100%. Bottom: of the transactions made in that hour, the share that is fraud.
What the data says
Fraud is spread almost evenly: every hour holds between 3.3% and 4.6% of it. Normal activity nearly stops overnight. So at 05:00, 58% of transfers and cash-outs are fraud, against 0.16% between 09:00 and 20:00.
What it means
Fraudsters are not more active at night; there are simply very few honest customers awake to hide among. A large transfer between 02:00 and 08:00 deserves a second look. Hours assume the simulation starts at midnight.

Q3

How much do they take, and do they empty the account?

More than double the normal amount, and almost always the whole balance.

How to read this chart
Left: how transaction sizes are distributed. The further right a curve sits, the larger the amounts. Right: the share of transactions that take everything the sender had.
What the data says
A typical (median) fraud transaction is 441,423, against 171,034 for a legitimate one: 2.6 times larger. 97.7% of fraud transactions empty the account, against 42.7% of legitimate ones.
What it means
The clearest warning sign is not the size of the transaction but that it takes the entire balance in one go.

Q4

If the bank checks only the riskiest transactions, how much fraud is stopped?

Checking the riskiest 1% catches 99.98% of fraud, in this simulation.

How to read this chart
A model gives every transaction a risk score. The bank cannot review everything, so it reviews only the highest-scoring ones. Each pair of bars is one review budget: red is the share of fraud cases inside that group, blue is the share of fraud money.
What the data says
Tested on a later period the model had not seen (552,504 transactions, 4,258 fraud). Reviewing the riskiest 1% (5,525 transactions) catches 99.98% of fraud cases, and 77% of those reviews turn out to be real fraud. Reviewing 0.5% catches 65%. A simpler model (logistic regression) catches 68% of cases at 1%.
What it means
A small, well-aimed review team can stop most fraud. But this result is far better than a real bank would see: the simulator creates fraud with a simple rule (take the whole balance), which a model learns almost perfectly.

Before you quote these numbers

  • The data is simulated. It is modelled on a real mobile-money service but is not real customer data and not Thai data.
  • Simulated fraud follows simple rules, so it is much easier to detect than real fraud. Treat Q4 as a demonstration of the method.
  • Amounts are in the simulation's currency units.

Data: PaySim, Lopez-Rojas, Elmir and Axelsson (2016), via Kaggle. Method: METHODOLOGY. Excel: results.xlsx.
